overgo dense block program on the device: a step of the block adapter beside the trainers it would replace
tool: OVERGO_CUDA_TEST=1 go test ./internal/densecausal -count=1 -v -run '^TestDenseBlockAdapterTrainsOnTheDevice$', on the change that follows commit cb23e6064
machine: one NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 D with nothing else on it, measured in a process of its own; host arithmetic on 32 workers
block: Qwen2.5-0.5B's shape (hidden 896, 14 heads of 64 over 2 shared, intermediate 4864) with seeded random weights, a sequence of 128 tokens, an adapter of rank 8 on all seven projections
read by: TestDenseBlockDeviceRecordIsWhole (internal/densecausal), which holds every figure to being here and every finding below to its figures

outcome: the block's forward graph and the backward graph derived from it run on the device as they
are, and train there as on the host: over ten updates of an adapter on a small block with grouped
attention every loss is the same to a part in a hundred thousand, and the loss falls. Beside the
trainers the program would replace the measurement is mixed.

Against the LoRA trainer, which is the only adapter trainer and runs on the host: a step of the
program on the device takes 8.7 ms where the LoRA trainer's share for a block is 165 ms, under an
eighteenth. That sets a device against a host. On the host itself, through the reference executor,
the program's step takes 337 ms, about twice the LoRA trainer's.

Against the resident trainer, which trains every weight on the device with a forward and backward
written by hand: with all seven projections adapted the program's forward and backward take 3.1 ms
and the resident trainer's share for a block 2.2 ms, so the derived passes take nearly half as long
again, though they differentiate by less (the factors, not the weights). With only the query's and
the value's projections adapted, the adapter most often trained, they take 1.9 ms, about what the
written ones take. The rest of a step is nearly all Muon's update of the factors on the host, the
update the LoRA trainer takes: 5.6 ms for fourteen factors and 1.1 ms for four. The whole step with all seven
adapted is shorter than the resident trainer's share for a block, 10.8 ms, most of which is its
update of every weight on the device. The program peaks at 119 MB of device memory (101 MB in
another run: the graph's order is not fixed), of which 60 MB are the block's weights and the rest a
step's activations and gradients; the resident trainer peaks at 305 MB on a model of one block and
adds 192 MB a block. It holds every weight's gradient and Muon's state on the device, which an
adapter's training does not, so the two memories are not one comparison.

Where the time goes. A step's graph is 260 operations, of which 205 are launched (the rest are
inputs and reshapes); the forward alone launches 46. Among them a step copies each weight it sends a
gradient through, transposed: about 56 MB a step, nearly all of it the output, gate, up and down
projections' weights, since a matrix product contracts over its weight's stored width and a gradient
through it contracts over the other. And a step moves the state, the target, the factors and their
gradients between host and device in 32 copies.

What was measured first, and changed. The attention's backward was first composed a query head at a
time: a step launched 517 operations, 364 of them attention's, and its passes took 4.6 ms, about
twice the written ones (commit 5b3c023a7 has that rule and that record). Replaying the launches as
one captured graph was tried there and did not shorten them: they were bound by the number of
operations, not by launching them. The rule now takes the query heads of a key head together, their
queries more columns of one matrix, so each of its products, its softmax and its mask is one
operation a key head: two passes of the rule where there were fourteen, with the operations that
exist.

How each figure was taken. Times are the mean of 64 steps on the device and 3 on the host, after one
step that is not timed. The LoRA trainer and the resident trainer train whole models, so each was run
on models of one and of five such blocks, and a block's share is a quarter of the difference; the
resident trainer was run once to load its kernels before the run measured, and its forward and
backward are its own synchronized measurement. Memory is the device's peak for the process's one
worker. Times are in microseconds and memory in bytes.
