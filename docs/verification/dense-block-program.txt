What was asked. A model's forward pass on the host, its forward pass on the device and its backward
pass are written apart, and a backward pass is written by hand for every trainer. The plan's row
shared-numerical-program asked whether one dense block could be run and trained from one
definition, a graph of the tensor program's operations with its backward graph derived from the
operations' own rules, and set five conditions for keeping it: the block agrees with the model's
own; its gradients agree with float64 directional derivatives; an adapter learns a controlled task
and resumes to the same values; device memory and time are no worse than the paths it replaces; and
production code is smaller once the rules are counted. If it was not accepted it was to be taken
out and its measurements kept.

What was built. A package of derivative rules, tensorgrad, that builds the graph of a scalar's
gradients from a forward graph out of the operations that exist, adding none: rules for a sum, a
product, a multiple, a matrix product, a transpose, a reshape, SiLU, RMS normalization, a softmax, a
rotary encoding in both layouts, and plain causal attention, grouped or not. A block adapter in the
dense model's package that writes one block once as such a graph, each adapted projection its
weight's product and the scaled product of two low-rank factors, derives the factors' gradients of
half the squared error against a target, and takes Muon's update of the factors. And its binding to
the device, where the same two graphs are compiled as they are and the weights are held.

How the acceptance read. Three conditions hold and two do not, so the program is not accepted.

The three that hold, on the host. With its factors at their start the program's block stands 3e-8
from the model's own layer forward, on a layer with grouped attention. Along a random direction
over everything differentiated at once, the backward graphs give what a float64 reference's central
difference gives: the feed-forward half 41.2819005 beside 41.2819439; a softmax over a projection
0.676451061 beside 0.676451318; causal attention with four query heads over two key heads
-1.27257174 beside -1.27257315, and over four 6.04152251 beside 6.04152457; and a whole block, by
its input and all seven weights, 227.779095 beside 227.779138. A rotary encoding's gradient is held
by its linearity. An adapter of rank two on the query and value projections, set a target the block
reaches under weights moved by an update of that rank, takes the loss from 0.000169318 to
0.00000876648 in sixty updates and the output from 0.053 of the target to 0.0095; an adapter that
resumes from another's kept factors and optimizer state takes the same next updates, to the bit. On
the device, over ten updates of an adapter on a small block with grouped attention, every loss is
the host's to a part in a hundred thousand, and the loss falls.

Time and memory, which does not hold. Beside the trainers the program would replace the measurement
is mixed, and where it is like for like it is against the program.

Against the LoRA trainer, which is the only adapter trainer and runs on the host: a step of the
program on the device takes 8.7 ms where the LoRA trainer's share for a block is 165 ms, under an
eighteenth. That sets a device against a host. On the host itself, through the reference executor,
the program's step takes 337 ms, about twice the LoRA trainer's.

Against the resident trainer, which trains every weight on the device with a forward and backward
written by hand: with all seven projections adapted the program's forward and backward take 3.1 ms
and the resident trainer's share for a block 2.2 ms, so the derived passes take nearly half as long
again, though they differentiate by less (the factors, not the weights). With only the query's and
the value's projections adapted, the adapter most often trained, they take 1.9 ms, about what the
written ones take. The rest of a step is nearly all Muon's update of the factors on the host, the
update the LoRA trainer takes: 5.6 ms for fourteen factors and 1.1 ms for four. The whole step with
all seven adapted is shorter than the resident trainer's share for a block, 10.8 ms, most of which
is its update of every weight on the device. The program peaks at 119 MB of device memory (101 MB in
another run: the graph's order is not fixed), of which 60 MB are the block's weights and the rest a
step's activations and gradients; the resident trainer peaks at 305 MB on a model of one block and
adds 192 MB a block. It holds every weight's gradient and Muon's state on the device, which an
adapter's training does not, so the two memories are not one comparison.

Where the time goes. A step's graph is 260 operations, of which 205 are launched (the rest are
inputs and reshapes); the forward alone launches 46. Among them a step copies each weight it sends a
gradient through, transposed: about 56 MB a step, nearly all of it the output, gate, up and down
projections' weights, since a matrix product contracts over its weight's stored width and a gradient
through it contracts over the other. And a step moves the state, the target, the factors and their
gradients between host and device in 32 copies.

What was measured first, and changed. The attention's backward was first composed a query head at a
time: a step launched 517 operations, 364 of them attention's, and its passes took 4.6 ms, about
twice the written ones (commit 5b3c023a7 has that rule and that record). Replaying the launches as
one captured graph was tried there and did not shorten them: they were bound by the number of
operations, not by launching them. The rule then took the query heads of a key head together, their
queries more columns of one matrix, so each of its products, its softmax and its mask is one
operation a key head: two passes of the rule where there were fourteen, with the operations that
exist.

Size, which does not hold. The program is 4,451 production nodes, 2,158 the rules, 1,989 the block
adapter and 304 its device binding, and it replaces nothing. The LoRA trainer is 2,813 and the
resident trainer 2,736. To replace both, the program would need what is not built, the weights' own
gradients, the optimizer on the device, the embedding, the head and the loss, on passes that are
slower.

What would change the reading. Two costs are the operations', not the rules': a matrix product that
could contract over its weight's other width would end the transposed copies, and an attention
backward over all heads in one operation would end the passes a key head. Both mean adding
operations to the tensor program, which the model-serving code imports: its serving records expire
and a full measurement run earns them again. The row ruled that out for a pilot. With them the
derived passes could meet the written ones; the size condition would still need both trainers
replaced whole.

A fault in the code at that commit. The RMS normalization's rule takes a row's rate from the
operation's own output, the row sum of output times output over the row sum of output times input.
For a row of zeros that is nothing over nothing, where the true gradient is the gradient over the
root of the epsilon (reviewer finding SQA-138, found by reading the rule and not by a run). The
measurements here used random rows and do not meet it. Whoever brings the rules back takes the rate
from the input and the epsilon.

What it found beside its question. An adapter's step on the device is far shorter than the LoRA
trainer's on the host, and adapters are trained on the host only. That does not need this program:
the resident trainer's written passes, with the weights' gradients projected to the factors as the
LoRA trainer projects them, would give it too. It is parked for the owner to rank, as finding
evidence:sha256:5b483098e0f0a3be8a8c22e68454fb31bc0e4db78b0f7d18c650bd6782b0181b.
